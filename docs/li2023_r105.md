# Li et al. (2023) baseline adaptation

> **Release inputs.** The compact three-run comparison is in `results/li2023_r105/`. Training regenerates the RDDPG checkpoints; the paper-data and audit scripts additionally require the corresponding frozen evaluation trajectories.

Ours is stored under the legacy key `IL` for reproduction compatibility.

Source: H. Li et al., *Constrained large-scale real-time EV scheduling based on recurrent deep reinforcement learning*, International Journal of Electrical Power & Energy Systems 144 (2023), 108603, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijepes.2022.108603. Dynamic energy boundaries and the nine-dimensional state are described on pages 3–4; the two replay buffers and episode-level updates are on pages 5–6; the reported hyperparameters are on page 7. The implementation is named **Li-RDDPG charging adaptation**, rather than a byte-for-byte reproduction of unavailable author code.

This experiment adapts Li et al.'s shared sequential RDDPG actor and dynamic energy boundary to the fixed-day, heterogeneous, unidirectional charging scenarios. The configured number of piles equals the scenario size and is known at the start of each experiment. The comparison does not use the manuscript's SPP for Li's policy. It uses the existing three runs of our approach and the identical 50 formal inputs at 600 and 800 kW. Additional fixed-day inputs use 1,000 and 3,000 piles, three seeds per size and 4 kW per pile.

The policy has nine inputs: current absolute hour divided by 14, price, urgency share G, pending minimum-power share H, previously allocated power share D, remaining hours divided by 14, SoC, and upper and lower power bounds divided by rated power. G normalizes `(target SoC - current SoC) / remaining hours` across connected EVs. Time normalization and the representation of a departed EV as a terminal zero state are implementation conventions. ReLU hidden layers, tanh actor output, Adam, and terminal masking are also implementation conventions; the paper does not specify all of these details.

The actor and critic each use hidden widths 64, 32, 16. Training uses 6,000 episodes, 10 piles, seeds 33001–33003 and common scenario seeds 900000–905999. Station limits cycle through 40, 35 and 30 kW. The reward retains Li's cost and absolute cost-fairness terms with beta 0.4, using `-price * power * 0.25` for the 15-minute energy cost and scale z=1. The common evaluation objective instead uses beta 0.3 and this repository's squared allocation unfairness; the training objectives therefore differ. There is no hyperparameter search or test-based checkpoint selection.

The replay buffer holds 60,000 transitions, batch size 64; the first five episodes supply D history and are not stored as transitions. From episode six, the next-state D is the mean at the same time and pile over the five preceding episodes. The 15-minute adaptation stores five complete 56-by-10 D grids, replacing the original hourly grid. Each later complete episode yields one actor, critic and target update. Learning rates are 0.0001 and 0.001, discount 0.99, soft update 0.01, and Gaussian exploration standard deviation starts at 2 and decays by 0.9993 each episode. The final 6,000-episode actor is used; intermediate actors are recorded every 500 episodes.

For each pile, initial risk power is `min(rated power, station limit / configured pile count)` and the risk start is calculated from the arrival-to-target energy requirement. When this start precedes arrival, the initial boundary is the greater of arrival SoC and the backward risk-power boundary at arrival; its excess over arrival is recorded as an initial boundary deficit. This is a conservative boundary deficit, not proof of physical infeasibility. During the risk interval, the rate is raised to at least the current `min(rated power, station limit / connected count)` and never reduced thereafter. Fractional risk starts use the overlap with each time slot. The boundary stops at target SoC. In the unidirectional trajectory, the local upper power bound is the lesser of rated power and remaining target energy divided by efficiency and slot length; this is equivalent to enforcing the forward upper envelope.

The paper's printed Eq. 18 is inconsistent with its Eq. 17. This adaptation uses the local nonnegative lower bound and limits the upper bound by station capacity remaining after previous allocations and subsequent piles' minimum demands. If the resulting interval is empty, the lower bound is set to the feasible upper bound and the event and its magnitude are recorded; departure targets are not guaranteed. The same check is recorded when constructing next states using estimated D. No additional safety projection is applied.

To train the main actor for 6,000 episodes in a fresh directory, run `uv run python scripts/run_li2023_r105.py --seed 33001 --output outputs/rddpg_33001`. Repeat with seeds 33002 and 33003 and distinct output directories for the training-variation study. The published checkpoint is `artifacts/runs/li2023_r105/33001/episode_6000.pt`. Across-run standard deviations are computed after averaging days within each run.

## Main-paper presentation

The main paper uses the prespecified final actor from seed 33001, while the separate three-seed summaries above describe training variation. The fixed actor of our approach is seed 11002. No test-set model selection is performed. The small-fleet comparison uses the same 20 archived expert inputs and the original 40 kW limit; the figure's typical day is seed 76000. The scale plots use the same three archived fixed-day inputs for each of 100, 500, 1000 and 3000 vehicles, with 4 kW per vehicle. The 200-vehicle table uses the 50 formal inputs at each capacity. `paper_rddpg_data.py` evaluates only the additional 10-, 100- and 500-vehicle inputs, verifies them against the archived scenarios, and reuses the frozen 200-, 1000- and 3000-vehicle trajectories. It writes the main table data to `results/li2023_r105/main_paper_summary.json` and `.csv`; zero unmet vehicles yield zero shortfall values in this table.

The main-paper comparison uses 20 small-fleet days, a typical day with seed 76000, and three fixed-day inputs for each larger fleet size. In the boxplots, `(method - UC) / UC` is used, so negative values represent reductions.

## Supplementary independent-training comparison

Three independent Li runs each completed 6,000 episodes, without hyperparameter search or test-set checkpoint selection. This supplementary comparison also uses three frozen runs of our approach. The 200-EV experiment has 50 common independent days per capacity; each large-fleet setting has three common independent days. Training runs are not counted as additional independent day scenarios. There are 318 Li evaluations and 18 additional large-fleet evaluations of our approach, with 300 formal evaluations of our approach reused from the existing training-repeat archive.

The following values average days inside each training run, then average the three run means. The full between-run sample standard deviations are in `results/li2023_r105/summary.csv`.

Unfairness in this table is the manuscript's squared normalized-power allocation metric. At 600 kW, our approach's mean total battery-energy shortfall is 0.195 kWh per 200-EV day, while the Li adaptation meets all departure targets. This service trade-off accompanies the cost and unfairness gains.

The independent audit also evaluates both methods using Li's cost-plus-absolute-cost-deviation criterion with beta 0.4. At 800 kW its loss is 1152.45 for our approach versus 1455.71 for the Li adaptation; at 600 kW it is 1185.41 versus 1586.39. The same ordering holds in the two large-fleet settings. This is a method-level comparison: learning paradigm, state representation, reward, network, and safety procedure all differ, so the results do not isolate the causal contribution of one component.

All saved trajectories satisfy charger, station, and SoC bounds up to floating-point residuals. The largest station residual is 1.874e-10 kW; the other audited power and SoC residuals are zero. Material empty DEB intervals (greater than 1e-8 kW) occur on average 7.26 and 52.60 times per 200-EV day at 800 and 600 kW, respectively. Their mean within-day shares of connected decisions are 0.114% and 0.824%. The capacity-respecting empty-interval rule and all such events are disclosed; observed target attainment is an empirical result, not a universal departure guarantee. Strict zero-tolerance interval counts are retained separately because floating-point accumulation can change those counts without changing the material classification.

`audit_li2023_r105.py` checks saved trajectories without new training or policy simulation. It verifies all six large-fleet inputs against the existing scenario archives and records their hashes. The six large-fleet trajectories from the original checkpoint of our approach also match the existing archive arrays exactly. Six targeted unit tests cover network/replay interfaces, reward units and terminal handling, dynamic-boundary activation and persistence, and empty-interval handling. An independent check of the three final actors found maximum NumPy-versus-PyTorch inference differences below 1e-6 on 100 fixed test vectors.
